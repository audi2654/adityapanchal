The hardest part of good work is often not the work itself. It is protecting the small, unremarkable conditions that let the work happen.

An empty hour is easy to underestimate. It does not look productive in a calendar. It cannot be forwarded in an email. But an hour without a meeting, notification, or pending reply is where a thought can become more than its first draft.

I am trying to treat attention as something I arrange rather than something I spend. That means leaving more space between commitments, keeping a shorter list, and admitting that the first task is rarely the most important one.

> The point of a quiet system is not to do less. It is to make room for the work that deserves more of you.

This is less dramatic than a new methodology. It is mostly subtraction: fewer open tabs, fewer automatic yeses, fewer decisions left half-made.

There is a practical test for whether a system is helping: after using it, do you have more clarity than before? If not, the system may be generating activity instead of reducing uncertainty.

I want my days to contain enough empty surface for the next good question to land.
